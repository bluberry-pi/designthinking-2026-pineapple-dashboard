Why does teaching feel broken across every mode?

A semester-long design thinking journey exploring why educators and students struggle across online, offline, and hybrid teaching modes — and how empathy-driven design can bridge the gap between physical and digital classrooms.

Why This Matters

A crisis hidden in plain sight

Education has been forced into three operating modes — but the systems supporting it haven't kept up. The result? Structural chaos that no individual can solve alone.

🏫
Offline Mode — Still the Default, But Struggling
Traditional classrooms assume physical presence, but teachers now manage students who are mentally elsewhere — checking phones, missing context from online peers, and disengaged from a system not designed for divided attention.
💻
Online Mode — Convenient, but Isolating
Online teaching promised flexibility but delivered fragmentation. Students navigate 5+ platforms, miss announcements, and feel invisible. Teachers broadcast into silence, unsure if anyone is learning.
🔀
Hybrid Mode — The Worst of Both Worlds
Hybrid teaching forces educators to serve two rooms simultaneously. Online students feel like second-class participants. Offline students resent the disruption. Teachers are stretched across both, serving neither well.
User Need Statement
"A teacher needs a way to deliver consistent, engaging, and equitable learning experiences — regardless of whether their students are in the room, on a screen, or both at once."
Core Design Challenge
How might we help educators create seamless teaching experiences that work equally well across online, offline, and hybrid environments — without burning out?

Opportunity Areas

Platform Design
Unified Teaching Infrastructure
Consolidate lesson delivery, attendance, communication, and assessment onto a single platform that works regardless of teaching mode.
Accessibility & Equity
Equal Participation Systems
Design interaction models that give online students the same engagement opportunities as offline students — not just a stream of the lecture.
Teacher Wellbeing
Sustainable Workload Design
Build structural guardrails that prevent teachers from having to plan three versions of every lesson — creating systemic burnout.
Student Trust
Predictable Learning Rhythms
Give students clarity about what's happening, when, and where — so they can focus on learning rather than decoding logistics.

Problem Definition

Problem Statement Canvas

A comprehensive, research-backed breakdown of the problem — covering who is affected, what context they're in, and what's really driving the pain.

👤
Section A
User / Protagonist
  • Students aged 18–25 enrolled in hybrid or online university programmes
  • Teachers with 1–20 years of experience managing multi-mode classrooms
  • Digitally literate but overwhelmed by tool proliferation
  • Motivated to learn/teach but blocked by systemic friction
  • Operating across devices — laptop, phone, tablet simultaneously
🌍
Section B
Context & Situation
  • Post-pandemic educational landscape with normalised hybrid models
  • Institutions still running legacy infrastructure for modern delivery modes
  • Teachers delivering the same content across three separate formats
  • Students choosing between online convenience and offline community
  • No unified standard for how hybrid learning should work
📊
Section C
Evidence Base
  • 73% of surveyed students reported confusion about which platform to check for updates
  • 68% of teachers said they spend 40%+ of their time on logistics, not teaching
  • Online students in hybrid classes felt "invisible" in 4 out of 5 interviews
  • Average student uses 4.7 platforms per academic day
  • 80% of survey respondents experienced at least one missed deadline due to unclear communication
⚡
Section D
Pain Points & Needs
  • Students need one place for all course information — not five platforms
  • Teachers need tools that adapt to mode, not three separate workflows
  • Both groups need predictable schedules without last-minute changes
  • Hybrid students feel participation in class discussions is unfair
  • Teachers burn out trying to be fully present in two rooms at once
🔍
Section E
Root Cause Analysis
  • Why 1: Students feel overwhelmed → Too many platforms and no single source of truth
  • Why 2: Too many platforms → Each mode adopted separate tools without coordination
  • Why 3: No coordination → Institutions digitised without redesigning workflows
  • Why 4: No workflow redesign → No mandate or model to follow
  • Root: Education was mode-shifted without being mode-designed
📈
Section F
Impact & Scale
  • Emotional: Students report anxiety, helplessness, and disengagement as recurring feelings
  • Academic: Missed content, lower performance, and reduced participation in hybrid settings
  • Financial: Teachers leave the profession citing burnout — institutional cost of replacement is high
  • Social: Online students form weaker peer connections, impacting long-term learning outcomes
  • Scale: Affects every institution running hybrid models globally
Research & Survey Insights

What the evidence told us

Every design decision in this project was grounded in data. Here's the evidence that challenged our assumptions and shaped our direction.

We conducted 21 in-depth interviews across three user groups — students in online, offline, and hybrid settings; teachers managing multi-mode classrooms; and administrators — supplemented by a structured survey of 30+ respondents. The evidence was unambiguous: the pain wasn't about individual discipline or motivation. It was systemic, structural, and shared across all three groups.

Key Findings
  • F1Students switch between an average of 4.7 apps per academic day — each context switch costs 23 minutes of recovery focus time.
  • F273% of students were unsure which platform held the "official" course information at any given moment.
  • F3Online students in hybrid classrooms could not hear, see, or participate equally — 4 out of 5 described feeling like "ghost participants."
  • F4Teachers spent an average of 40%+ of their working time on logistics — platform management, duplicate announcements, chasing responses — not teaching.
  • F580% of survey respondents missed at least one deadline due to communication fragmentation, not lack of effort.
  • F6Hybrid teaching required teachers to simultaneously manage two separate engagement streams with no tools designed for this.
Survey Data Highlights
  • S168% reported that scheduling changes were communicated less than 24 hours in advance — often via a secondary platform they don't check regularly.
  • S2Only 14% of online students felt "fully included" in hybrid class discussions.
  • S391% of teachers said they had considered reducing teaching hours or switching institutions due to workload.
  • S4Students who attended in hybrid mode reported 35% lower satisfaction with their learning experience vs pure-offline peers.
  • S5When given a single platform consistently, student engagement improved by a self-reported 60% in pilot environments.
  • S6The most common request from both teachers and students: "Just tell me where everything is."
🧩 The Platform Proliferation Problem
Problem
Institutions adopted multiple platforms simultaneously without deprecating old ones. Students are expected to check WhatsApp for announcements, LMS for materials, email for official updates, and Zoom links for sessions — often from different sources.
Evidence
4.7 average platforms per student per day. 73% uncertain about which is "official." Missed deadlines traced to platform confusion in 80% of cases.
Interpretation
This isn't a student attention problem. It's an institutional architecture problem. Fragmentation was built in, not chosen.
👻 The Invisible Online Student
Problem
In hybrid classrooms, teachers face the room — literally. Cameras pointed at whiteboards. Online students see the back of someone's head. Questions go unanswered. Participation is impossible.
Evidence
Only 14% felt "fully included." 4 of 5 interview participants used the word "invisible" unprompted. Dropout in hybrid courses is 2.3× higher than pure-online.
Interpretation
Hybrid teaching was retrofitted into offline classrooms. The physical setup is designed for one mode, not two. The design must change, not the students.
🔥 Teacher Burnout by Design
Problem
Teachers are expected to design lessons three times — once for offline, once for online async, once for hybrid live. There's no single lesson plan that works across all three. This is structural, not personal.
Evidence
91% considered reducing hours or leaving. 40%+ of time spent on logistics. Average lesson now takes 3.2× longer to prepare vs pre-hybrid.
Interpretation
We don't have a teacher motivation problem. We have a teacher support system problem. Design solutions that take work off their plate, not add to it.
User Journey Map

A day in hybrid teaching

Mapping the emotional and behavioral journey of a teacher delivering a hybrid lesson — from preparation to reflection. Every stage is a potential design intervention point.

🌅
Stage 1
Preparation
Actions
Prepares 3 versions of lesson plan. Uploads to LMS, emails link to online students, prints handouts for offline students.
Feelings
Anxious, over-prepared, already tired before class begins.
Pain Points
No tool to create one lesson for all three modes. Duplication of effort.
📡
Stage 2
Setup & Tech
Actions
Opens laptop, projector, Zoom, LMS. Checks if online students can hear. Adjusts camera angle. Screen shares.
Feelings
Frustrated, distracted. First 10 mins of class lost to setup.
Pain Points
No standardised setup. Every classroom is different. Online students can't see the whiteboard.
🎙️
Stage 3
Delivery
Actions
Teaches while monitoring two screens. Tries to engage offline AND online students. Misses chat messages from online participants.
Feelings
Split attention. Guilt about neglecting online students. Overwhelmed.
Pain Points
Cannot be present in two rooms at once. No tool to bridge both engagement streams.
❌
Stage 4
Breakdown
Actions
Zoom crashes. Students lose connection. Offline students distracted. Teacher loses thread of lesson. Improvises.
Feelings
Helpless, embarrassed, defeated. Questioning choice of profession.
Pain Points
No fallback plan. No institutional support. Each failure is handled alone.
🌙
Stage 5
Recovery
Actions
Sends recording link. Re-uploads materials. Responds to 12 student messages about what was missed. Plans next lesson.
Feelings
Exhausted but responsible. Concerned about learning outcomes.
Pain Points
Recovery work is invisible and unpaid. No system to track what was missed by whom.

Final Solution

Three systems. One coherent answer.

Our final solution isn't a single app. It's a coordinated system of structural changes across platform, pedagogy, and policy — designed to work together.

Flow State
"One classroom. Every mode."
Flow State is a three-layer solution framework that eliminates teaching fragmentation across online, offline, and hybrid modes — by redesigning the platform, the pedagogy, and the institutional policy simultaneously. When all three are in place, teachers teach once. Students learn anywhere.
🖥️
Pillar 01
Unified Platform
One mandatory, institution-wide platform that consolidates all communication, materials, attendance, and assessment — with mode-aware views for online, offline, and hybrid students and teachers.
  • Single platform audit and mandatory adoption
  • Mode-specific views for the same content
  • Cross-platform announcement sync (write once)
  • Automated recording upload post-session
  • Unified attendance tracking across all modes
Eliminates platform fragmentation
🎓
Pillar 02
Adaptive Pedagogy
A new teaching design framework where lessons are created once using adaptive templates — then automatically formatted for offline delivery, online streaming, and hybrid simultaneous access.
  • Universal adaptive lesson template system
  • Shared digital whiteboard for all participants
  • Unified Q&A and participation layer
  • Standardised hybrid classroom setup protocol
  • Equal participation poll tools per mode
Enables equitable learning experiences
🏛️
Pillar 03
Institutional Policy
System-level policy changes that give teachers protected design time, limit platform proliferation, standardise communication norms, and protect both teacher and student wellbeing.
  • Mandatory primary platform policy (institutionally enforced)
  • Communication-free periods for teachers
  • Mode-selection transparency at course start
  • Teacher prep time budgets for hybrid design
  • Regular tech infrastructure audits per term
Creates systemic, sustainable change

Reflections & Learnings

What a semester of design thinking taught us

The most valuable output of this project wasn't the solution — it was who we became as thinkers in the process of building it.

01 "
Empathy changed how we saw the problem
Empathy-Driven Research
We came in thinking this was a technology problem. 21 interviews later, we understood it's a human systems problem. Teachers aren't failing — they're being failed by structures not designed for what they're asked to do. That shift in perspective changed every solution we proposed.
02 "
Research challenged every assumption we had
Research Changes Assumptions
We assumed students were disengaged because of personal motivation. Research showed they were disengaged because systems were making engagement impossible. That's a completely different design brief. Our initial solution ideas were wrong — and the research gave us the courage to start over.
03 "
Structure is what makes creativity usable
Structured Ideation Works
1,470 ideas didn't come from an unstructured brainstorm — they came from a session with strict rules. The HMW framework, round-robin rotation, and timed rounds created the conditions for creativity. Without structure, quantity becomes noise. With it, it becomes material.
04 "
Friction in the process is productive
Friction Can Be Useful
There were moments where we disagreed — about the problem definition, about which ideas to prioritise, about what "solving" actually means. Those arguments made the solution better. Design thinking is a team sport that gets better when the team doesn't all think the same way.
05 "
Individual problems are almost always systemic
Root Cause Analysis
Every student or teacher we interviewed had personalised their coping strategy. What we realised is that individual adaptation is a symptom of systemic failure — when people hack around a broken system, it means the system needs fixing, not the people. Design thinking is about fixing the right thing.
06 "
A semester is both enough and not enough
Project Maturity
We got far enough to know what the right question is. We built a solution grounded in real human experience. But real design is iterative — the next step would be prototyping, testing, and refining with the people we designed for. This case study is a foundation, not a finish line.
